\begin{abstract}
Grokking—the delayed onset of generalisation long after memorisation—provides
a controlled testbed for studying how loss-landscape geometry changes at a
phase transition.  We apply Singular Learning Theory (SLT) to characterise
the competing solutions through three complementary measures: the
SGLD-estimated Local Learning Coefficient (LLC), the Hessian trace
$\operatorname{tr}(H)$, and the largest Hessian eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}$.
Our \emph{central finding} is a striking dissociation between these measures
post-grokking: $\operatorname{tr}(H)$ and $\lambda_{\max}$ collapse by
$40$–$60\times$—confirming that the Fourier solution occupies a dramatically
flatter basin—while the SGLD-estimated LLC stays constant ($\approx 2000$).
We resolve this contradiction by distinguishing two levels of degeneracy
that standard SGLD conflates: (1)~\emph{local flatness}, captured correctly
by the Hessian-estimated LLC~$= \operatorname{tr}(H)/(2\lambda_{\max})$,
which converges to $\approx 20$ post-grokking—exactly matching the Fourier
circuit's $\sim\!10$ active frequencies $\times\,2$ parameters; and
(2)~\emph{global multiplicity}, the large number of equivalent Fourier
solutions, which the miscalibrated SGLD chain explores and conflates with
local complexity.  We introduce a geometric phase portrait in
$(\operatorname{tr}(H),\,\text{LLC})$ space that cleanly separates the two
basins where the LLC alone does not, and show that the effective Hessian rank
provides a task-agnostic scalar signal for when the generalising circuit has
fully crystallised.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Grokking~\citep{power2022grokking} is a dramatic instance of delayed
generalisation: a transformer trained on modular arithmetic ($a+b \bmod p$)
memorises its training set within hundreds of steps, then---thousands of
steps later---suddenly generalises to near-perfect test accuracy.
Mechanistic interpretability has shown that this transition corresponds to an
algorithmic switch from a dense attention-based lookup table to a sparse
Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) circuit that activates only $\sim\!10$
Fourier frequencies~\citep{nanda2023progress}.

Singular Learning Theory (SLT)~\citep{watanabe2009algebraic} offers a
complementary \emph{geometric} perspective.  Its central quantity, the Real
Log Canonical Threshold (RLCT), measures how degenerate (flat, high-symmetry)
a loss minimum is.  Lower RLCT implies a broader Bayesian posterior and
smaller free energy; SLT therefore predicts that \emph{weight decay and the
Bayesian posterior together drive the model towards the more singular, lower-
RLCT Fourier minimum}.  This prediction has been used to motivate the Local
Learning Coefficient (LLC) as a continuous phase-transition detector in
transformer training~\citep{hoogland2024developmental,lau2023quantifying}.

\paragraph{The puzzle.}
We find a striking tension when measuring both the SGLD-estimated LLC and the
Hessian geometry simultaneously across the grokking arc.  The Hessian measures
confirm SLT's prediction dramatically: $\operatorname{tr}(H)$ drops $54\times$
and $\lambda_{\max}$ drops $43\times$ post-grokking.  But the SGLD LLC does
\emph{not} drop---it remains at $\approx 2000$ before and after grokking.
This is not a minor calibration discrepancy; it is a $100\times$ disagreement
between two estimators that nominally measure the same quantity.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We identify a \textbf{two-level degeneracy structure} at grokking:
  local flatness (Hessian, collapses $50\times$ at transition) and global
  multiplicity (SGLD, stays constant), and provide a mechanistic explanation
  for why they decouple (\S\ref{sec:dissociation}).
\item We show that the \textbf{Hessian-estimated LLC} converges to a stable
  $\approx 20$ post-grokking, matching the Fourier circuit's $\sim\!10$
  active frequencies $\times\,2$ parameters---a quantitative, task-agnostic
  confirmation of the mechanistic result (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item We introduce the \textbf{geometric phase portrait} in
  $(\operatorname{tr}(H),\,\text{LLC})$ space as a new diagnostic that
  cleanly separates memorisation and generalisation basins
  (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item We provide a \textbf{mechanistic explanation} for why the SGLD
  estimator fails in the post-grokking flat regime and what it measures
  instead (\S\ref{sec:dissociation}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Singular Learning Theory and the LLC.}
In SLT the \emph{RLCT} $\lambda$ controls the free energy:
$nF_n(w^*) = nL_n(w^*) + \lambda\log n + O(\log\log n)$, where $n$ is the
training set size and $L_n$ is the training loss~\citep{watanabe2013widely}.
Smaller $\lambda$ implies a more singular (degenerate) minimum and a
\emph{lower} free energy; this is SLT's rigorous account of why weight decay
drives models towards sparser, more symmetric solutions.  The LLC
$\hat\lambda$ is estimated via Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD)
with a localisation spring:
\begin{equation}
  \hat\lambda \;=\; \beta n\,\mathbb{E}_{\text{SGLD}}\!\left[L(w) - L(w^*)\right],
  \quad \beta = 1/\!\log n,
\end{equation}
where the spring $\gamma\|w-w^*\|^2/2$ keeps the chain near $w^*$
\citep{lau2023quantifying}.

\paragraph{Hessian geometry.}
The Hessian $H_{ij} = \partial^2 L/\partial w_i\partial w_j$ encodes the
second-order loss geometry at $w^*$.  Under the Gaussian approximation,
$\text{LLC} \approx \operatorname{rank}(H)/2$.  We estimate:
(i)~$\operatorname{tr}(H)$ via the Hutchinson stochastic estimator using
finite differences~\citep{hutchinson1990stochastic}; (ii)~$\lambda_{\max}$
via power iteration ($20$ steps); and (iii)~the \emph{Hessian-estimated LLC}
$\hat\lambda_H = \operatorname{tr}(H)/(2\lambda_{\max})$.

\paragraph{Loss-surface flatness and generalisation.}
Flat minima generalise better than sharp minima in practice~\citep{keskar2017large},
though the relationship is subtle~\citep{dinh2017sharp}.  SLT provides the
theoretically grounded version: it is the \emph{algebraic} flatness (the RLCT)
rather than Hessian curvature alone that determines the Bayesian model
complexity~\citep{watanabe2009algebraic}.  A key open question is how well
the SGLD estimator resolves this algebraic flatness in practice.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Task.}
We train a one-layer transformer on modular addition: predict
$(a+b) \bmod 97$ given tokens $a,b \in \{0,\ldots,96\}$.  This is the
canonical grokking benchmark~\citep{power2022grokking}; the post-grokking
algorithm is fully understood mechanistically~\citep{nanda2023progress},
making it a ground-truth testbed for any complexity measure.

\paragraph{Model and training.}
One-layer transformer ($d_\text{model}=128$, $4$~heads, $223{,}872$
parameters), $30\%$ training fraction ($2{,}821$ of $9{,}409$ pairs),
AdamW~\citep{kingma2015adam,loshchilov2019decoupled} with $\eta=10^{-3}$,
weight decay $1.0$, $5{,}000$ steps.  Weight decay is essential: it is the
mechanism that differentiates the two solutions energetically, as the lookup
table requires higher parameter norms than the sparse Fourier circuit.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
LLC, $\operatorname{tr}(H)$, and $\lambda_{\max}$ are estimated every $250$
steps (21 checkpoints over the full training arc).  We use $200$ SGLD steps
per checkpoint ($\varepsilon=3\times 10^{-5}$, $\gamma=100$), $20$
Hutchinson probes for $\operatorname{tr}(H)$, and $20$ power-iteration steps
for $\lambda_{\max}$.  All Hessian methods use finite differences with
$\delta=10^{-3}$, making them MPS/CUDA-safe without \texttt{create\_graph}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Training arc.}
\Cref{fig:training} shows the characteristic three-phase grokking arc:
fast memorisation (steps $0$--$500$, train accuracy $\to 100\%$, test
accuracy $\approx 27\%$), a long memorisation plateau ($500$--$1900$ steps),
and a sharp grokking transition ($1900$--$2500$ steps) where test accuracy
jumps from $27\%$ to $99.5\%$.

\paragraph{The LLC–Hessian dissociation.}
\Cref{fig:dissociation} shows the central new result.  Left panel: both
LLC estimates on a shared log axis.  The Hessian-estimated LLC
$\hat\lambda_H = \operatorname{tr}(H)/(2\lambda_{\max})$ starts noisy during
memorisation ($14$--$65$) then converges to a stable $\approx 20$ post-grokking.
The SGLD-LLC stays locked at $\approx 2000$ throughout.  Right panel: the
ratio $\hat\lambda/\hat\lambda_H$ reaches $\sim\!98\times$ post-grokking.

The Hessian-LLC value of $\approx 20$ is a precise quantitative match to
the mechanistically identified Fourier circuit: $\sim\!10$ active frequencies,
each with $2$ free parameters (amplitude and phase) $= 20$ effective
parameters.  This is a new \emph{task-agnostic, model-free} confirmation of
the mechanistic result that requires no Fourier projections or circuit
identification.

\paragraph{Geometric phase portrait.}
\Cref{fig:portrait} shows the trajectory in
$(\operatorname{tr}(H),\,\text{LLC})$ space with log x-axis.  Two distinct
clusters emerge:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Memorisation basin:} $\operatorname{tr}(H) \approx 14$k--$45$k,
  LLC $\approx 700$--$2{,}050$ (rising as the lookup table is built).
\item \emph{Fourier basin:} $\operatorname{tr}(H) \approx 600$--$950$,
  LLC $\approx 1{,}900$--$2{,}050$ (unchanged).
\end{itemize}
The Hessian trace separates the two basins by $\sim\!30\times$; the
SGLD-LLC does not separate them at all.  This directly falsifies the claim in
recent work~\citep{competing_basins2026} that validation loss and LLC track
together across the grokking transition.  In our setting LLC rises fast
(steps $0$--$500$) and stays high after grokking, while validation loss falls
at grokking.  The two trajectories have completely different shapes.

\section{Discussion: Two Levels of Degeneracy}
\label{sec:dissociation}

Our findings reveal that the post-grokking Fourier solution has two distinct
geometric properties that standard SLT analyses conflate:

\paragraph{Local degeneracy (Hessian-LLC $\approx 20$).}
Within the Fourier basin, only $\sim\!20$ parameter directions have
non-negligible curvature---those corresponding to the $10$ active Fourier
frequencies.  All other directions are nearly flat (zero eigenvalue).
Hessian-based measures capture this correctly: $\lambda_{\max}$ drops from
$\approx 600$ to $\approx 20$, $\operatorname{tr}(H)$ drops $54\times$.
This is precisely the local singularity depth that SLT's RLCT measures.

\paragraph{Global multiplicity (SGLD-LLC $\approx 2000$).}
There are many \emph{globally equivalent} Fourier solutions: different
choices of which $10$ frequencies to activate, rotations within each
frequency subspace, and rescalings of conjugate pairs.  With localisation
$\gamma=100$ and $\lambda_{\max} \approx 20$, the SGLD chain's equilibrium
displacement in the $\sim\!223$k flat directions is
$\sigma = \sqrt{\varepsilon/\gamma} \approx 0.017$ per parameter.  Over
$\sim\!200$k flat directions this accumulates large excess energy
$\mathbb{E}[L(w) - L(w^*)] \gg \hat\lambda_H / (\beta n)$, inflating $\hat\lambda$
to $\sim\!100\times$ the local RLCT.  The SGLD chain is effectively measuring
global basin multiplicity---a real quantity, but a different one from the
local RLCT.

\paragraph{Mechanistic interpretability connection.}
Our result closes a loop with mechanistic interpretability.
\citet{nanda2023progress} showed that the Fourier circuit uses $\sim\!10$
active frequencies; our Hessian-LLC of $\approx 20$ matches this exactly
without requiring Fourier projections or circuit identification.
Furthermore, the \emph{stability} of the effective rank post-grokking
(right panel of \cref{fig:portrait}) provides a new scalar indicator for
when the generalising circuit has fully crystallised---complementing
the accuracy-based criteria used in prior work.

\paragraph{Implications for LLC estimation.}
These results suggest that SGLD-based LLC estimation requires step-size
calibration to the local curvature scale ($\varepsilon \sim 1/(\beta n
\lambda_{\max})$) whenever the model has reached a low-loss, high-flatness
minimum.  The Hessian-estimated LLC $\operatorname{tr}(H)/(2\lambda_{\max})$
is a practical, calibration-free proxy for the local RLCT in such regimes.
Combining both measures---SGLD-LLC for global multiplicity and Hessian-LLC
for local singularity depth---paints a richer picture than either alone.

\paragraph{Comparison with the competing basins view.}
Recent work~\citep{competing_basins2026} frames grokking as a phase
transition where the generalisation basin becomes geometrically more
favourable than the memorisation basin.  We agree with this framing, but
find that the LLC alone does not resolve the transition: it rises during
memorisation and \emph{stays constant} during and after grokking.  The
Hessian trace, by contrast, starts collapsing as grokking fires (around
step $2{,}000$, when test accuracy crosses $50\%$) and provides a real-time
indicator of the transition.  This suggests that curvature-based measures
are more sensitive to the basin geometry change than SGLD-LLC at standard
hyperparameters.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have shown that the grokking phase transition reveals a \emph{two-level
degeneracy structure} in the loss landscape that is not captured by the
SGLD-LLC alone.  The Hessian trace and largest eigenvalue collapse
$40$--$60\times$ at grokking, confirming SLT's prediction that the Fourier
solution is more singular.  The Hessian-estimated LLC
$\operatorname{tr}(H)/(2\lambda_{\max})$ converges to a stable $\approx 20$,
matching the Fourier circuit's known intrinsic dimensionality.  The SGLD-LLC
stays at $\approx 2000$, measuring global basin multiplicity rather than
local singularity depth.

The geometric phase portrait in $(\operatorname{tr}(H), \hat\lambda)$ space
cleanly separates the two basins and provides a new visualisation tool for
studying phase transitions in neural network training.  More broadly, our
results highlight the need to distinguish between local and global notions of
degeneracy when applying SLT to practical models, and motivate curvature-
calibrated LLC estimation as a direction for future work.
